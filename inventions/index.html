A field guide to human ingenuity

Spark

Twelve inventions, five and a half millennia. Each one looked small at first — a spinning disc, a floating needle, a moldy dish — and each one rewired the world. Scroll to watch the fuse burn.

Scroll

The Ancient Worldc. 3500 BCE – 1500 CE

c. 3500 BCE

The Wheel

Mesopotamia · Sumerian craftspeople

The first wheels didn’t carry anything — they spun clay on potters’ tables. When Mesopotamian craftspeople turned one on its side and fixed it to an axle, hauling changed forever: a load that took a team of people to drag could roll behind a single ox. Trade stretched farther, cities fed themselves from distant fields, and nearly every machine since — gears, turbines, hard drives — still turns on the same idea.

Han dynasty, c. 2nd century BCE

The Compass

China · Han diviners, later described by Shen Kuo

Han dynasty diviners noticed that spoons carved from lodestone always swung to rest pointing the same way. Centuries later, Chinese sailors floated magnetized needles in water — and suddenly the open ocean had signposts. Freed from hugging coastlines or waiting for starry nights, ships could cross whole seas under cloud. The compass made sea charts, global trade routes and the age of exploration possible, and it still steers every map app today.

c. 105 CE

Paper

Luoyang, Han China · Cai Lun

Court official Cai Lun refined a pulp of bark, hemp and rags into thin, cheap sheets — and knowledge slipped its heavy bindings of bamboo, silk and stone. Ideas could now be copied, carried and stored by the thousand. The craft traveled the Silk Road, reaching Samarkand by 751 and then Europe through the Islamic world. Books, exams, contracts, letters, money: civilization began keeping its memory on paper.

1440s

The Printing Press

Mainz, Germany · Johannes Gutenberg

China and Korea printed first — Bi Sheng’s movable type around the 1040s, Korea’s metal-type Jikji in 1377. But Gutenberg’s screw press, oil-based ink and cast metal letters turned printing into an industry. A book that took a monk a year could be printed in days, and prices collapsed. Literacy spread, the Reformation caught fire, and science gained its superpower: exact copies of knowledge, everywhere at once.

Movable type was an East Asian invention long before Europe’s press.

The Industrial Age1700s – 1800s

1712 · 1769

The Steam Engine

England & Scotland · Thomas Newcomen, James Watt

Thomas Newcomen’s 1712 engine pumped water out of flooded coal mines — hungry and wasteful, but it worked. James Watt’s separate condenser of 1769 made steam efficient enough to power almost anything. For the first time, humanity owned muscle that never tired and didn’t depend on wind, water or horses. Factories left the riverbanks, railways and steamships shrank the globe, and the Industrial Revolution roared into motion.

The Electric Age1830s – 1950s

1837

The Telegraph

USA & Britain · Morse & Vail · Cooke & Wheatstone

Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail in America — and Cooke and Wheatstone in Britain — taught electricity to carry language down a wire. Before 1837, information traveled only as fast as a horse or a ship; afterward, it moved at nearly the speed of light. Dots and dashes stitched continents together, a transatlantic cable followed in 1866, and news, markets and empires began running on the same clock.

1879

The Light Bulb

Menlo Park, USA & Newcastle, England · Edison & Swan

Edison in New Jersey and Joseph Swan in England raced to the same answer: a carbon filament glowing inside a vacuum. Edison’s deeper genius was the system around the bulb — generators, meters and wiring to light whole districts at once. Night stopped ending the working day. Homes, streets and factories were wired for electricity, and the grid built for light ended up powering everything invented since.

1928

Penicillin

London & Oxford · Fleming · Florey, Chain & Heatley

Back from holiday, Alexander Fleming found a stray mold killing bacteria in a forgotten culture dish. A decade later, Florey, Chain and Heatley turned that lucky observation into a mass-produced drug, just in time for World War II. Infections that had killed throughout history — battlefield wounds, childbirth fever, a scratched knee gone bad — became curable in days. Antibiotics made modern surgery and medicine survivable.

1947

The Transistor

Bell Labs, New Jersey · Bardeen, Brattain & Shockley

In December 1947 at Bell Labs, Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley coaxed a sliver of germanium into amplifying an electrical signal. The transistor replaced hot, fragile vacuum tubes with a tiny, rugged switch that sips power. It became the most manufactured object in human history — a modern chip packs billions — and every computer, phone and satellite is, at heart, an ocean of transistors flicking on and off.

The Digital Age1960s – today

1969 → 1989

The Internet

UCLA & CERN · ARPANET team · Cerf & Kahn · Berners-Lee

In 1969, ARPANET’s first message — meant to say LOGIN — crashed after two letters: “LO”. From that stumble grew a network of networks, standardized by Cerf and Kahn’s TCP/IP in 1983. Then, in 1989, Tim Berners-Lee gave it a human face: the World Wide Web. Knowledge, commerce and conversation moved onto one shared wire, reachable from anywhere on Earth.

2007

The Smartphone Era

Cupertino, USA · Apple, after IBM’s Simon (1994)

Handheld computers already existed — IBM’s Simon dialed in 1994, BlackBerrys ruled boardrooms — but 2007’s iPhone fused phone, internet and a multitouch screen into something anyone could use, and app stores soon let it become anything. For billions of people, a phone was their first computer. Camera, map, bank, library and post office collapsed into a slab of glass that fits in a pocket.

The Genomic Age2012 – …

2012

CRISPR Gene Editing

Berkeley & Umeå · Jennifer Doudna & Emmanuelle Charpentier

Doudna and Charpentier showed in 2012 that a bacterial immune system could be reprogrammed into precise molecular scissors for DNA. Gene editing went from years of specialist labor to weeks in an ordinary lab. The pair won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and by 2023 the first CRISPR medicine — Casgevy, for sickle cell disease — was approved. Editing life’s source code is now a tool, and a responsibility.

The next spark is unwritten.
