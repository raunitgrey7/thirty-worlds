Color Theory
Playground

Pick one base color. Six classic harmonies are computed live on the HSL wheel — click any swatch to copy its hex, then stress-test your pairings against WCAG contrast standards below.

Complementary

Two hues sitting directly opposite each other on the wheel produce the strongest possible color contrast. Designers reach for it when one element — a call-to-action, a price tag, a warning — must pop against everything else; let one hue dominate and keep its opposite as a small, deliberate accent.

Analogous

Three neighbors on the wheel share an undertone, so the scheme reads calm, cohesive and almost inevitable — think forest greens sliding into teal. It suits editorial layouts, wellness brands and backgrounds where mood matters more than emphasis, but you will need lightness contrast to build hierarchy.

Triadic

Three hues spaced evenly at 120° give vibrancy with built-in balance — the classic comic-book and children's-brand recipe. It works best when one color leads and the other two are demoted to accents; used at equal strength, all three fight for attention.

Split-complementary

Instead of the exact opposite, you take the two hues flanking it — most of a complement's punch with far less tension. It is the scheme most often recommended to beginners because it is genuinely hard to make ugly, and it leaves you two accent options instead of one.

Tetradic

Four hues arranged as two complementary pairs create the richest — and riskiest — palette in classical theory. It shines in data visualization and illustration where many categories need distinct colors; tame it by letting neutrals carry most of the surface area.

Monochrome

One hue stepped through lightness, from near-white to near-black, can never clash with itself — harmony is guaranteed. It is the backbone of elegant, typography-led interfaces; the trade-off is that emphasis must come from scale, weight and spacing rather than from color.
