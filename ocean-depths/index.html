A journey in five zones

Into
the Deep

The ocean is a vertical world. Scroll down and you will sink 11,000 metres — past the last ray of sunlight, past the Titanic, all the way to the deepest place on Earth. Everything you meet on the way down is real.

↓ Scroll to begin your descent

0 – 200 m

The Sunlight Zone

Epipelagic — “upon the sea”

Warm, bright and busy. This thin skin of water — thinner, proportionally, than the peel on an apple — is the only layer where there is enough light for photosynthesis, so nearly all of the ocean’s food is made here. Around 90% of all marine life lives in this zone, and tropical surface water can reach a bath-like 30 °C.

40 m

The recommended limit for recreational scuba diving. Beyond this, ordinary air starts to act strangely on the human body.

🐢

Green sea turtle

Chelonia mydas

Grazes seagrass meadows like an underwater cow, and can hold its breath for several hours while resting. Females navigate thousands of kilometres of open ocean to lay eggs on the very beach where they hatched.

🦈

Whale shark

Rhincodon typus

The largest fish on Earth — commonly 10–12 m long — yet it eats almost nothing but plankton, filtering it through its metre-wide mouth. Each shark’s pattern of white spots is unique, like a fingerprint.

🪼

Moon jellyfish

Aurelia aurita

About 95% water, with no brain, heart, bones or blood — and it has drifted through the seas in roughly this form since long before the dinosaurs, more than 500 million years.

🐟

Sardine

Sardinops sagax

Safety in numbers, taken to extremes: sardines school in shoals of many millions. South Africa’s annual “sardine run” is one of the largest movements of animal biomass anywhere on the planet.

200 m

Light fades to about 1% of its surface strength. Photosynthesis becomes impossible. The open ocean’s “surface” world ends here.

200 – 1,000 m

The Twilight Zone

Mesopelagic — “middle sea”

A blue dusk that never becomes day. No plants can grow, so everything here either hunts, scavenges the “marine snow” drifting down from above, or commutes: every night, billions of animals rise hundreds of metres to feed near the surface and sink again by dawn — the largest migration on Earth, and it happens daily. Most residents make their own light.

214 m

The deepest a human has ever free-dived on a single breath — Herbert Nitsch, “no limits” record, 2007.

332 m

The deepest scuba dive in history (Ahmed Gabr, 2014). The descent took 12 minutes; returning safely took nearly 14 hours.

🏮

Lanternfish

Family Myctophidae

Possibly the most abundant vertebrates on Earth. Their nightly rise to the surface is so dense that early sonar operators mistook the layer of fish for a false seafloor.

🐠

Marine hatchetfish

Argyropelecus spp.

Mirror-silver sides and rows of belly photophores tuned to match the faint light above — “counter-illumination” camouflage that erases its silhouette from predators looking up.

🦑

Vampire squid

Vampyroteuthis infernalis

Neither a vampire nor a true squid. It drifts in water almost empty of oxygen, eats drifting marine snow, and when startled turns its webbed arms inside-out over its head like a spiky umbrella.

🐡

Barreleye

Macropinna microstoma

Has a transparent, fluid-filled head. Its green tube-shaped eyes sit inside the dome and rotate upward to spot prey silhouetted against the dim light far above.

535 m

The deepest recorded dive of an emperor penguin — the deepest-diving bird in the world.

1,000 – 4,000 m

The Midnight Zone

Bathypelagic — “deep sea”

Total, permanent darkness — not one photon of sunlight has ever reached below 1,000 m. The water hovers around 4 °C and the pressure exceeds 100 atmospheres. The only lights are living ones: an estimated three-quarters of the animals here can produce their own bioluminescent glow.

1,000 m

The last, faintest trace of sunlight is gone. From here to the seafloor, the ocean has been dark for its entire 4-billion-year history.

🎣

Humpback anglerfish

Melanocetus johnsonii

The female’s glowing lure is a colony of luminous bacteria on a modified fin ray. The male is a tiny fraction of her size; in some anglerfish species he fuses to her body for life, sharing her bloodstream.

🦑

Giant squid

Architeuthis dux

Grows to 12–13 m and has the largest eyes in the animal kingdom — about 27 cm across, the size of dinner plates — for catching the faintest flickers of light. First filmed alive in the deep only in 2012.

🐍

Gulper eel

Eurypharynx pelecanoides

Mostly mouth: its pelican-like jaws can engulf prey larger than its own body, and the tip of its whip-thin tail carries a glowing organ thought to lure prey close in the dark.

🐋

Sperm whale

Physeter macrocephalus

A visitor from the surface that dives past 2,000 m on breath-holds over an hour long, hunting squid by sonar. Its echolocation clicks reach ~230 decibels — the loudest sound made by any animal.

2,992 m

The deepest dive ever recorded for any mammal — a Cuvier’s beaked whale, tracked in 2014.

3,700 m

You pass the ocean’s average depth (~3,700 m). Roughly half of all the seawater on Earth lies deeper still.

3,800 m

The wreck of RMS Titanic rests here on the North Atlantic floor, discovered in 1985 — 73 years after she sank.

4,000 – 6,000 m

The Abyss

Abyssopelagic — “bottomless sea”

Named from the Greek for “bottomless”. Near-freezing water, crushing pressure, and vast plains of soft mud: the abyssal plains that begin here are the largest single habitat on Earth, covering more than half the planet’s surface. Food is so scarce that many residents simply wait for it to fall from above.

🐙

Dumbo octopus

Grimpoteuthis spp.

The deepest-living octopus known, filmed near 7,000 m. It swims by flapping two ear-like fins, and has lost its ink sac entirely — ink is useless where there is no light to hide from.

🐟

Tripod fish

Bathypterois grallator

Stands motionless on three stilt-like fin rays, facing into the current with feeler-fins raised, waiting for plankton to drift into reach. Its eyes barely work — it hunts by touch.

🐖

Sea pig

Scotoplanes globosa

A pink, plump sea cucumber that walks the mud on inflatable tube-feet “legs”. Sea pigs gather in herds, all facing the same direction, plowing the seafloor for freshly fallen organic matter.

🦷

Fangtooth

Anoplogaster cornuta

Has the largest teeth of any fish relative to its body size — so long that it has special sockets beside its brain so the mouth can close. Fearsome looking, but only about 16 cm long.

6,000 m

The abyssal plains end. Only ocean trenches plunge deeper — and a whole hidden world lives in them.

6,000 – 11,000 m

The Hadal Zone

Hadopelagic — named for Hades, god of the underworld

The deep beyond the deep: found only in long, narrow trenches where one tectonic plate dives beneath another. Pressure here climbs past 1,000 atmospheres — over a tonne pressing on every square centimetre — yet even the deepest trench floor is alive.

6,500 m

The operating limit of most crewed research submersibles, including the upgraded American sub Alvin — famous for exploring the Titanic.

🐟

Hadal snailfish

Pseudoliparis spp.

The deepest fish ever seen: one was filmed at 8,336 m in Japan’s Izu-Ogasawara Trench in 2023. Soft, scaleless and faintly pink, it survives thanks to pressure-stabilising molecules in its cells.

🦐

Giant hadal amphipod

Hirondellea gigas

Shrimp-like scavengers that swarm by the thousand on the floor of the Challenger Deep. They coat their shells in an aluminium-hydroxide gel — home-made armour against the crushing, corrosive depths.

🦠

Xenophyophore

e.g. Syringammina

Among the largest single cells on Earth — one cell the size of your fist. These fragile giants glue sediment into elaborate shelters and carpet parts of the trench floor.

🐟

Cusk-eel

Abyssobrotula galatheae

Trawled from 8,370 m in the Puerto Rico Trench in 1970, it held the “deepest fish” record for half a century — and it still holds the record for the deepest fish ever physically caught.

8,336 m

The deepest any fish has ever been observed. Below roughly 8,400 m, scientists believe fish bodies simply cannot work.

10,935 m

Challenger Deep, Mariana Trench — the deepest surveyed point in all the world’s oceans.

You’ve reached the bottom.

This is Challenger Deep: 10,935 metres down. If Mount Everest were dropped in here, its summit would still be more than 2 km underwater. The pressure is about 1,086 bar — the weight of a small car concentrated on your thumbnail.

Humans first touched this floor on 23 January 1960, when Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh rode the bathyscaphe Trieste down in a dive that took nearly five hours. Film director James Cameron repeated the feat solo in 2012. Until 2019, more people had walked on the Moon than had ever been here.

And yet, even here, the mud stirs with amphipods, sea cucumbers and giant single-celled life. We have better maps of Mars than of our own seafloor — over 75% of it has never been mapped in high resolution. The deep is not empty. It is simply unvisited.
