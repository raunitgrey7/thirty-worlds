Festivals of the World

One year on Earth, ten ways to celebrate it. Scroll through the calendar — from the first new moon of spring to the last fireworks of December.

Begin the year▼
January – February

Lunar New Year

  • WhereChina, Vietnam (Tết), Korea (Seollal) & communities worldwide
  • WhenFirst new moon of the lunisolar year

The most important reunion of the year begins on the first new moon of the lunisolar calendar. Families travel vast distances for a New Year's Eve reunion dinner; homes are cleaned to sweep out bad luck, couplets go up in red, and elders hand children red envelopes of lucky money. Firecrackers and lion dances drive off misfortune — legend says the beast Nian feared noise and the color red — and fifteen days later, the Lantern Festival closes the season.

March

Holi

  • WhereIndia & Nepal, and South Asian communities worldwide
  • WhenFull moon of the month of Phalguna

On the eve, the bonfires of Holika Dahan burn away evil, recalling the devotee Prahlad saved from the flames. Next morning every street becomes a canvas: friends and strangers dust each other with gulal — powder in fuchsia, saffron and green — soak passersby with water, and share gujiya pastries and cool thandai. For a day, distinctions of age, class and rank blur. Holi celebrates spring's arrival, the playfulness of Radha and Krishna, and forgiveness — old grudges are meant to wash off with the color.

Late March – April

Hanami

  • WhereJapan, as the blossom front moves north
  • WhenRoughly a week per town, when the sakura peak

Hanami, “flower viewing,” is less a festival than a national pause. As the sakura zensen — the cherry-blossom front — sweeps up the archipelago, forecasters track it like weather, and friends, families and coworkers claim picnic spots under blooming trees, staying into lantern-lit yozakura evenings. The blossoms hold for barely a week before scattering, and that is the point: they embody mono no aware, the gentle ache of impermanence, savored with bento boxes, sake and falling petals.

April 13 – 15

Songkran

  • WhereThailand, nationwide
  • WhenThe traditional Thai New Year

Thailand's New Year begins with quiet rituals: Buddha images are bathed in scented water, and younger people pour water over their elders' palms to ask a blessing. Then the cleansing spills outward — streets across the country erupt into the world's largest friendly water fight, with buckets, bowls and water guns dousing everyone in reach. The water carries the old year's misfortune away. Homes are cleaned, sand stupas are raised at temples, and in 2023 UNESCO recognized Songkran as intangible cultural heritage.

1 Shawwal · moves with the moon

Eid al-Fitr

  • WhereMuslim communities worldwide
  • WhenThe day after Ramadan ends, about 11 days earlier each year

The “festival of breaking the fast” arrives with the sighting of the new crescent moon. Before the morning prayer, every household gives zakat al-fitr, a charity ensuring no neighbor goes without a holiday meal; then congregations gather in mosques and open fields, dressed in their finest. The day belongs to visiting — embraces of “Eid Mubarak,” children receiving gifts and Eidi money, and tables of dates, ma'amoul and sheer khurma, sweets so central that many simply call it Sweet Eid.

June 24

Inti Raymi

  • WhereCusco, Peru
  • WhenThe southern winter solstice season

In the shortest days of the Andean year, the Inca honored Inti, the sun, asking him to return. Banned by Spanish colonial authorities in 1572, the Festival of the Sun was revived in 1944 from colonial chronicles and is now Cusco's greatest day: hundreds of performers in Inca regalia process from the Qorikancha, the golden temple of the sun, to the fortress of Sacsayhuamán, where the Sapa Inca invokes the sun in Quechua before crowds tens of thousands strong.

Mid-August

Obon

  • WhereJapan (mid-July in parts of Tokyo)
  • WhenThree days when ancestors visit home

For a few summer days, the ancestors come home. Families light mukaebi welcoming fires and lanterns to guide the spirits back, clean family graves, and set offerings at household altars. Neighborhoods gather for Bon Odori — circle dances in cotton yukata around a raised yagura stage, to taiko drums. When the visit ends, send-off fires burn — Kyoto ignites giant characters on its mountainsides — and in tōrō nagashi, paper lanterns drift downriver, carrying the spirits gently back.

November 1 – 2

Día de los Muertos

  • WhereMexico, and Mexican communities abroad
  • WhenAll Saints' and All Souls' days

Death arrives as an honored guest. Families build ofrendas — home altars layered with cempasúchil marigolds, candles, photographs, sugar skulls, pan de muerto and the favorite dishes of those being remembered — because the scent of marigolds is said to guide souls home. Cemeteries fill with music, stories and candlelight through the night. Blending Indigenous Mesoamerican rites with the Catholic calendar, it is remembrance without gloom: for one night each year, the dead return to celebrate with the living.

October – November

Diwali

  • WhereIndia, South Asia & the diaspora; also observed by Jains, Sikhs and Newar Buddhists
  • WhenThe new moon of Kartika

The festival of lights fills the darkest night of the lunar month with rows of clay diyas on windowsills and rooftops. For many Hindus it marks Rama and Sita's return to Ayodhya, welcomed home by lamplight; households also honor Lakshmi, goddess of prosperity, with cleaned homes and rangoli patterns at the door. Five days run from Dhanteras to Bhai Dooj, filled with new clothes, mithai sweets, fireworks — and the shared conviction that light outlasts darkness.

December 31 – January 1

Hogmanay

  • WhereScotland
  • WhenNew Year's Eve into Ne'erday

Scotland gives New Year's Eve its own name and takes it seriously — for centuries after the Reformation, when Christmas was suppressed, Hogmanay was the great winter feast. At midnight, strangers link arms for Auld Lang Syne; then comes first-footing, when the first visitor across your threshold brings coal, shortbread or whisky for luck. Edinburgh throws one of the world's great street parties with a torchlight procession through the Old Town, and in Stonehaven, locals swing blazing fireballs down the high street.

And then the year begins again.
