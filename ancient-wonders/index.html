Antipater of Sidon’s list · 2nd century BC

The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World

Seven monuments so audacious that travelers wrote them into legend. Six are gone. One still stands on the Giza plateau. Scroll to walk the list.

Begin at Giza▼
Wonder I · The Survivor

Great Pyramid of Giza

Era
Old Kingdom Egypt
Location
Giza plateau, Egypt
Built
c. 2560 BC, for Pharaoh Khufu
Fate
Still standing — the only wonder that survives

For nearly four thousand years this was the tallest structure humans had ever built. Some 2.3 million blocks of limestone and granite were cut, hauled and set with astonishing precision by organized crews of paid laborers — not slaves, as legend claims. Originally cased in polished white Tura limestone that flashed in the sun, the Great Pyramid was engineered to carry King Khufu into eternity. Alone among the seven, it remains.

Wonder II · The Mystery

Hanging Gardens of Babylon

Era
Neo-Babylonian Empire
Location
Babylon, near modern Hillah, Iraq
Built
c. 600 BC — if they existed at all
Fate
Never conclusively located; lost to time

The most mysterious wonder may never have existed. Greek writers described terraces of trees and cascading greenery — a mountain of gardens said to be built by Nebuchadnezzar II for his wife Amytis, homesick for the green hills of Media. Yet Babylonian records never mention them, and no ruins have been found. One modern theory relocates the gardens to Nineveh, where the Assyrian king Sennacherib built spectacular watered gardens a century earlier.

Wonder III · The Masterwork

Statue of Zeus at Olympia

Era
Classical Greece
Location
Sanctuary of Olympia, Greece
Built
c. 435 BC, by the sculptor Phidias
Fate
Lost to fire in the 5th century AD

In the temple at the home of the Olympic Games sat Zeus enthroned — nearly thirteen meters of gold and ivory over a wooden frame, holding Victory in one hand and an eagle-topped sceptre in the other. Visitors joked that if the god ever stood up, he would take the roof off. Archaeologists later found Phidias’ workshop, tools and a cup bearing his name. The statue itself vanished, most likely destroyed by fire — at Olympia, or after removal to Constantinople.

Wonder IV · The Twice-Built

Temple of Artemis at Ephesus

Era
Archaic to Hellenistic Greece
Location
Ephesus, near Selçuk, Türkiye
Built
c. 550 BC; rebuilt after 356 BC
Fate
Destroyed by Goths, 262 AD; one column stands

Rebuilt in ever greater splendor, the marble house of Artemis stretched longer than a modern football field, ringed by over a hundred columns nearly eighteen meters tall. In 356 BC a man named Herostratus burned it purely so his name would be remembered; legend says the goddess was away that night, attending the birth of Alexander the Great. Antipater of Sidon, who compiled the wonder list, wrote that beside this temple every other marvel dimmed.

Wonder V · The Namesake

Mausoleum at Halicarnassus

Era
Late Classical; the satrapy of Caria
Location
Bodrum, Türkiye
Built
c. 350 BC
Fate
Toppled by earthquakes, 12th–15th centuries

When Mausolus, ruler of Caria, died, his wife and sister Artemisia II raised him a tomb so magnificent that it gave every grand tomb since its name: mausoleum. Forty-five meters tall, it stacked a colonnade and a stepped pyramid upon a great podium, crowned by a marble four-horse chariot, with friezes by four of the most celebrated Greek sculptors of the age. Medieval earthquakes brought it down, and the Knights of St John quarried its stones for Bodrum Castle.

Wonder VI · The Bronze Giant

Colossus of Rhodes

Era
Hellenistic Greece
Location
Harbor city of Rhodes, Greece
Built
292–280 BC, by Chares of Lindos
Fate
Felled by earthquake in 226 BC, after 54 years

To celebrate surviving a great siege, the Rhodians sold the abandoned siege machinery and raised a bronze colossus of their patron sun god Helios — some thirty-three meters tall, the height of the Statue of Liberty heel to crown. It never straddled the harbor; that image is medieval fantasy. After only fifty-four years an earthquake snapped it at the knees, and the fallen giant lay where it broke for eight centuries, still drawing awed visitors, before being sold for scrap.

Wonder VII · The Beacon

Lighthouse of Alexandria

Era
Ptolemaic Egypt
Location
Pharos island, Alexandria, Egypt
Built
c. 280 BC, under Ptolemy I and II
Fate
Ruined by earthquakes, 956–1323 AD; stones reused in the Citadel of Qaitbay

On the island of Pharos rose a tower of pale stone over a hundred meters tall — square at the base, octagonal in the middle, round at the top — its fire and great mirror said to be visible dozens of kilometers out to sea. It guided ships past Egypt’s treacherous shoals for over a thousand years, so successfully that “pharos” became the very word for lighthouse in many languages. Earthquakes finally felled it; divers still find its huge blocks on the harbor floor.

Six fell. One endures. All are remembered.

The list itself is the eighth wonder — twenty-three centuries old and still setting itineraries. Part of the Thirty Worlds collection.
